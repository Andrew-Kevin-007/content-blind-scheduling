\begin{abstract}
Large language model (LLM) inference services schedule requests without knowing
how many tokens each will generate. Because a long response occupies a batch
slot for many iterations, first-come-first-serve (FCFS) scheduling suffers
head-of-line blocking, and a substantial literature therefore predicts output
length from the prompt text in order to approximate shortest-job-first. Reading
prompt content is precisely what a privacy-constrained or regulated serving
platform cannot do. We quantify what that restriction costs. Analysing 44.1
million requests from Microsoft Azure's production LLM inference traces, we
observe that service work splits into a prefill component, which is exactly
observable at admission from the context length alone, and a decode component,
which is not observable until the request completes, and that the observable
component is the larger one: prefill is 91.2\% and 98.7\% of attributable
marginal work, and 76\% and 92\% of measured engine time at high load. We
evaluate CB-SJF-Work, a scheduler that orders admissions by estimated total
service work using only token counts and arrival metadata. Over 20
(conversation) and 11 (code) windows sampled across a full production week, it
reduces mean normalised latency by 51.5\% and 58.8\% relative to FCFS and
recovers 90.3\% and 79.3\% of the improvement attainable by an oracle with
perfect knowledge of output length. Two findings qualify this. First, the
benefit is not free: shortest-first ordering defers long requests, and
99th-percentile latency degrades by up to 1.9$\times$ (conversation) and
3.1$\times$ (code) at the highest load, a cost that an oracle scheduler incurs
equally. Second, a learned content-blind output-length predictor contributes
almost nothing over ordering by raw context length (0.1\% and 1.3\%), and
ordering by predicted output length alone is worse than FCFS on the code
workload. For these workloads the information a scheduler is denied turns out
to be the information it least needs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Serving large language models is now a dominant consumer of cloud GPU capacity.
A modern inference engine such as vLLM~\cite{kwon2023pagedattention} or
Orca~\cite{yu2022orca} executes requests with \emph{continuous batching}: many
requests share a batch, each scheduling iteration emits one token for every
resident request, and a request holds its slot and its share of the key-value
(KV) cache until it produces its final token.

This creates a scheduling problem with an unusual information structure. How
long a request holds its slot is proportional to its output length, and that
length is unknown when the request arrives. Under first-come-first-serve (FCFS),
the default in production engines, one long-generating request admitted early
blocks many short ones behind it, producing head-of-line blocking and long tails.

The established response is to predict output length and approximate
shortest-job-first (SJF). $S^3$ fine-tunes a DistilBERT model on the prompt to
predict sequence length~\cite{jin2023s3}; Fu \emph{et al.} learn to rank
requests by predicted output length, again from prompt text~\cite{fu2024ltr}.
Both report large gains, and both require the scheduler to read the user's
prompt. That is a genuine obstacle: a platform under data-protection
obligations, serving regulated sectors, or offering confidential-computing
guarantees may be unable to route prompt content into a scheduling component,
and running an auxiliary transformer over every prompt consumes the accelerator
capacity the scheduler is trying to conserve. Hence our question: how much of
the achievable scheduling benefit remains available to a scheduler that never
reads prompt content?

We answer it empirically on Microsoft Azure's public production LLM inference
traces~\cite{azurepublicdataset}, which contain 44.1 million real requests
across a conversation workload and a code workload. Our central finding is that
the question's premise, that the valuable information is locked inside the
prompt, does not hold for these workloads. Service time has two components: a
prefill pass over the input tokens, whose cost is fixed by the context length
and therefore visible at admission without reading any content, and a sequence
of decode iterations, whose cost is not. On these traces prefill is the larger
component under either of the two accountings we report in
Section~\ref{sec:characterisation}.

We make the following contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
\item A quantification of what content-blindness costs an admission scheduler.
      Ordering by observable work recovers 90.3\% (conversation) and 79.3\%
      (code) of the improvement an output-length oracle attains, evaluated over
      20 and 11 windows sampled across a full production week
      (Sections~\ref{sec:setup}--\ref{sec:results}).
\item A characterisation of the observable and unobservable components of
      service work on 44.1M production requests, reported under two accountings
      so the conclusion does not rest on one cost convention, together with the
      finding that output length is weakly predictable from content-blind
      metadata for conversational traffic ($\rho_s = 0.365$) and effectively
      unpredictable for code completion ($\rho_s = 0.107$)
      (Section~\ref{sec:characterisation}).
\item Two negative results. A learned content-blind length predictor adds
      almost nothing over ordering by raw context length, and ordering by
      predicted output length alone is worse than FCFS on the code workload.
      Under a content-blindness constraint, the effort of learning to predict
      output length is not repaid.
\item An accounting of the cost side. Every work-aware or length-aware policy,
      including both oracles, degrades tail latency relative to FCFS. We report
      the magnitude rather than the mean-latency gain alone.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Continuous-batching inference engines.}
Orca~\cite{yu2022orca} introduced iteration-level scheduling, and
vLLM~\cite{kwon2023pagedattention} added paged KV-cache management, together
establishing the execution model we assume: a batch whose membership changes
every iteration, bounded by GPU memory available for the KV cache.
Sarathi-Serve~\cite{agrawal2024sarathi} reduces prefill-induced decode stalls by
chunking prefills, and Splitwise~\cite{patel2024splitwise} and
DistServe~\cite{zhong2024distserve} disaggregate the two phases onto separate
hardware. These works change where and how prefill and decode execute;
they are orthogonal to, and composable with, the question of which
request to admit next, which is our subject.

\textbf{Output-length prediction for scheduling.}
$S^3$~\cite{jin2023s3} predicts output length with a fine-tuned DistilBERT to
reserve KV memory more tightly. Fu \emph{et al.}~\cite{fu2024ltr} observe that
exact lengths are unnecessary and learn only the relative rank of output
lengths, reporting substantial latency reductions. Both extract their signal
from prompt text, which is the constraint we remove.

\textbf{Scheduling on input length.}
We are not the first to key scheduling on input length. FastServe's skip-join
MLFQ~\cite{wu2026fastserve} assigns each arrival an initial queue from its
profiled prefill time, and absorbs the unknown output length through
iteration-level preemption rather than prompt inspection.
PecSched~\cite{pecsched} analyses an Azure LLM inference trace, reports the same
skewed input-length distribution we observe, and preemptively prioritises
short-input requests at cluster scale. Both are prior art for content-blind,
input-length-keyed scheduling, and both obtain their tail behaviour from
preemption, which our non-preemptive setting excludes. Our question is narrower:
given that output length is unobservable, how much of the oracle-attainable
benefit does a scheduler forfeit?

\textbf{Production trace analysis.}
Characterising real cloud workloads has repeatedly overturned assumptions formed
on synthetic ones, as with Resource Central for VM
allocation~\cite{cortez2017resource}. The Azure LLM inference
traces~\cite{azurepublicdataset} were released alongside
Splitwise~\cite{patel2024splitwise} and DynamoLLM~\cite{stojkovic2025dynamollm}.
Splitwise already reports that the coding workload is prompt-dominated, with a
median of 13 generated tokens, and we do not claim that observation as new. What
we add is its consequence for admission scheduling: how the observable and
unobservable components of service work divide, and what a scheduler forfeits by
seeing only the former.

\section{Characterising Production LLM Traffic}
\label{sec:characterisation}

\subsection{Traces}
We use the Azure LLM Inference Trace 2024~\cite{azurepublicdataset}, released
under CC-BY-4.0/MIT. It contains two one-week traces from Microsoft's production
inference service: a conversation workload (27.3M requests) and a
code workload (16.8M requests). Each record gives an arrival timestamp,
the input context length in tokens, and the number of tokens generated. Crucially
for our purpose, the traces contain no prompt text: they are themselves
a content-blind view of the workload, which is exactly the information a
privacy-constrained scheduler would possess.

\subsection{The observable component is the larger one}
In a continuous-batching engine, servicing a request costs a prefill pass
proportional to its context length $x_i$, plus $y_i$ decode iterations where
$y_i$ is its output length. Charging decode at its marginal per-token cost
(the per-iteration base cost is a property of the batch, not of any one
request), the work attributable to request $i$ is

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:work}
  W_i \;=\; c_f \;+\; c_p\,x_i \;+\; c_d\,y_i ,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $c_p$ is the per-token prefill cost, $c_d$ the marginal
per-token decode cost, and $c_f$ a fixed overhead. The first two terms are known
at admission; only the third requires knowing $y_i$.

Evaluated over both full traces, \eqref{eq:work} puts prefill at 91.2\% of
attributable marginal work in the conversation trace and 98.7\% in the code
trace. The reason is the traffic shape. Mean context lengths are 1632 and 2511
tokens against mean output lengths of only 106 and 23, giving aggregate
context-to-output token ratios of 15.5:1 and 110.7:1. That ratio is a property
of the traffic and involves no cost model at all.

Equation~\eqref{eq:work} is an attribution convention, not a measurement of
engine time, because it charges decode only its marginal cost and assigns the
per-iteration base cost to the batch rather than to any request. Since our
argument rests on this split, we also report the other natural accounting:
the fraction of busy time our simulated engine actually spends in prefill
steps. That fraction is load-dependent, because larger batches amortise the
base decode cost over more requests. At $\rho=0.60$ it is 53.9\% (conversation)
and 81.8\% (code); at $\rho=0.92$ it is 75.6\% and 92.4\%. The two accountings
differ, most visibly for conversation at low load, and we report both rather
than the more favourable one. Under either, prefill is at least half of the
work and the component hidden from the scheduler is the smaller one.

\subsection{Output length is not equally predictable}
The unobservable component is also unequally guessable. Binning each trace by
context-length decile, mean output length rises roughly ninefold across the
deciles of the conversation trace, so context length carries real information
about output length there. For code completion the relationship is flat and
output length is statistically independent of input length. A content-blind
predictor (Section~\ref{sec:method}) attains a rank correlation of $\rho_s =
0.365$ with true output length on conversation but only $0.107$ on code, with
pairwise ordering accuracies of 0.633 and 0.537 against a chance level of 0.5.

The two workloads therefore have opposite risk profiles. The code trace has both
the heavier output-length tail and the weaker predictive signal: it is the
workload that would benefit most from length-aware scheduling and the one where
predicting length is hardest. A method that depends on accurate length
prediction is weakest exactly where it is most needed.

\section{Content-Blind Work-Aware Scheduling}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Threat model and information constraint}
The scheduler may observe, per arriving request, the context length in tokens,
the arrival timestamp, the workload class, and aggregate statistics over
requests that have already completed. It may not observe prompt or response
text. This corresponds to a front end that tokenises and meters requests, which
it must do for billing regardless, but forwards content only to the model.

\subsection{Policy}
Section~\ref{sec:characterisation} implies that ordering by predicted output
length optimises the wrong quantity. What determines contention is total service
work $W_i$, and by \eqref{eq:work} the observable terms dominate it.
CB-SJF-Work therefore admits requests in increasing order of

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:key}
  \widehat{W}_i \;=\; c_f \;+\; c_p\,x_i \;+\; c_d\,\hat{y}_i ,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\hat{y}_i$ is a content-blind estimate of output length. Because
$c_d \hat{y}_i$ contributes only a small share of $\widehat{W}_i$, the policy
degrades gracefully: when $\hat{y}_i$ is uninformative, the ranking is still
governed by the exactly-known prefill term.

We obtain $\hat{y}_i$ from a gradient-boosted regression tree trained on
$\log(1+y)$ over ten content-blind features: context length and its logarithm,
workload class, hour of day, day of week, inter-arrival time, recent arrival
rate, and rolling mean and 90th-percentile output length and mean context length
over the previous 512 completed requests. Rolling statistics are shifted by one
request, so no request observes its own or any future outcome, and an automated
check fails the run if any feature exceeds 0.95 rank correlation with the
target. The predictor trains on a contiguous earlier period of the same trace,
disjoint from every evaluation window, and is applied unchanged thereafter.

Admission is head-of-line: if the highest-priority waiting request does not fit
in the KV cache, the scheduler waits rather than scanning past it. The KV budget
for a model with $L$ layers, $H$ KV heads and head dimension $d$ at $b$ bytes
per element is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:kv}
  B \;=\; \left\lfloor (M_{\mathrm{gpu}} - M_{w}) \,/\, (2 L H d b) \right\rfloor
\end{equation}
tokens, where $M_w$ is the weight footprint. Note that the KV reservation in
Algorithm~\ref{alg:cbsjf} charges the true output length $y_i$, not the estimate
$\hat{y}_i$. This is a simplification: reserving on an underestimate would
require the preemption and recompute path that real engines use when a
reservation is exceeded, which we do not model. It applies identically under
every policy, including FCFS and both oracles, so it confers no advantage on any
of them. The scheduler's ordering key, which is what this paper studies, remains
strictly content-blind.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
  \caption{CB-SJF-Work admission (one scheduling iteration)}
  \label{alg:cbsjf}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require pending min-heap $Q$, running set $R$, KV budget $B$, used $u$
    \State \textbf{on arrival of} $i$: $\;$push $i$ into $Q$ with key
           $\widehat{W}_i$ from \eqref{eq:key} \Comment{content-blind}
    \While{$Q \neq \emptyset$}
      \State $i \gets \textsc{Peek}(Q)$
      \If{$|R| < B_{\max}$ \textbf{and} $u + x_i + y_i \le B$}
        \State $\textsc{Pop}(Q)$; $R \gets R \cup \{i\}$; $u \gets u + x_i + y_i$
      \Else
        \State \textbf{break} \Comment{head-of-line; do not scan past}
      \EndIf
    \EndWhile
    \State run prefill for newly admitted requests, else one decode iteration
    \State evict completed requests from $R$ and release their KV
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Methodology}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Simulator.} We built a discrete-event simulator of one
continuous-batching replica with iteration-level scheduling and the KV bound of
\eqref{eq:kv}. Prefill cost is affine in prompt tokens, decode cost affine in
batch size, with parameters for a 13B-parameter model ($L=40$, $H=40$, $d=128$,
fp16) on an 80\,GB accelerator, giving $B=65{,}917$ tokens. The cost model is
analytical, not measured on our hardware; Section~\ref{sec:limits} reports a
sensitivity analysis.

\textbf{Load calibration.} The published traces are fleet-aggregate (75--87
req/s), far above single-replica capacity. We therefore measure each window's
saturated throughput $\mu$ by driving the engine with an unbounded backlog, then
rescale the arrival timeline so that $\rho = \lambda/\mu$ takes the values
$\{0.60, 0.75, 0.85, 0.92, 0.98\}$. This preserves the arrival process shape and
request mix up to an affine rescaling of the clock. Measured capacity is 1.79
req/s (conversation) and 1.41 req/s (code).

Two caveats follow, because $\rho$ is easy to misread. First, $\mu$ is measured
at maximum concurrency, where the per-iteration base cost amortises over a full
batch, whereas the runs operate at smaller batches; measured utilisation
therefore exceeds $\rho$ substantially, reaching 0.91 (conversation) and 0.71
(code) at $\rho=0.60$. Read $\rho$ as a control variable, not as utilisation.
Second, this does not bias the comparison: throughput is identical across
policies to four significant figures.

\textbf{Windows.} We evaluate the first 20{,}000 requests of each of 20
(conversation) and 11 (code) windows sampled at even intervals across the full
week, each at least two hours from the next and after the predictor's training
period. Twenty thousand requests span roughly 4 to 13 minutes of arrivals, so a
window is a few minutes of traffic. We do not select busy windows: because
arrivals are rescaled to a target $\rho$, a window's absolute arrival rate is
normalised away and window choice affects only the request mix, so sampling
across the week costs nothing and covers diurnal variation. Replaying the same
windows under every policy makes all comparisons paired. The leading 10\% of
each run is discarded as warm-up.

\textbf{Policies.} We compare FCFS (the engine default); SJF-Ctx, which orders
by context length alone; SJF-Pred, which orders by predicted output length (the
objective of prior work, realised content-blind); our CB-SJF-Work; and two
oracles: Oracle-SJF, ordering by true output length, and Oracle-Work,
ordering by true total work. The oracles require information unavailable
at admission and are upper bounds, not deployable systems.

\textbf{Metrics.} Mean normalised latency $\bar{L}_n$ (end-to-end latency per
output token, the standard continuous-batching metric), 99th-percentile
end-to-end latency, and SLO attainment, defined as the fraction of requests
meeting both a 2\,s time-to-first-token and a 100\,ms time-between-tokens target.

Table~\ref{tab:recovery} quantifies how much of the
attainable benefit survives the content-blind restriction. Averaged over
$\rho \ge 0.85$, CB-SJF-Work recovers 90.3\% of the Oracle-Work improvement on
the conversation workload and 79.3\% on code. The improvement is positive in
every window at every load above $\rho=0.75$ (one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank,
$p < 10^{-3}$).

Two cautions on reading these percentages. The headline improvement is a ratio
of means, so it is weighted by whichever window has the worst FCFS run; the
per-window range in Table~\ref{tab:recovery} is wide (6\% to 77\% on
conversation at $\rho \ge 0.85$) and the mean of per-window improvements is
47.0\%, not 51.5\%. Second, the recovered-gap percentage is a ratio of two
quantities that both approach zero at low load, so its high value at $\rho=0.60$
describes a setting where the absolute improvement is under 1\% and should not
be read as success.

\subsection{The mean-latency gain is paid for in the tail}
Shortest-first ordering reduces the mean by deferring long requests, so it must
lengthen the tail. We report that cost rather than leave it to be inferred from
Table~\ref{tab:main}. On code, CB-SJF-Work has a worse 99th-percentile latency
than FCFS in 31 of 33 window-load cells at $\rho \ge 0.85$, by a mean factor of
2.05$\times$, rising to 3.12$\times$ at $\rho=0.98$. On conversation the tail is
essentially unchanged up to $\rho=0.92$ (0.98$\times$ and 1.00$\times$) and
degrades to 1.86$\times$ at $\rho=0.98$.

This cost is not attributable to content-blindness. Oracle-Work incurs it
equally, with $P_{99}$ ratios against FCFS of 1.42$\times$, 1.95$\times$ and
3.30$\times$ on code at the three highest loads, slightly worse than ours, and
Oracle-SJF is worse still (1261\,s against our 556\,s at $\rho=0.92$). The
penalty is a property of shortest-first admission, not of the information the
scheduler is denied. An operator who cares primarily about the tail should not
deploy any of these policies without an anti-starvation mechanism; preemptive
schedulers~\cite{wu2026fastserve,pecsched} address exactly this.

\subsection{The learned predictor earns almost nothing}
CB-SJF-Work differs from SJF-Ctx only by the $c_d \hat{y}_i$ term. Averaged over
$\rho \ge 0.85$ that term is worth 0.12\% on conversation and 1.30\% on code,
and is statistically indistinguishable from zero in every cell except code at
$\rho=0.98$ (one-sided Wilcoxon, $p=0.009$, positive in 9 of 11 windows, worth
3.45\%). At $\rho=0.92$ the two differ by 506 against 505 (conversation) and
6327 against 6337 (code). Ordering by raw context length is, for practical
purposes, as good as ordering by a learned estimate of total work, and we would
not recommend deploying the predictor for this purpose.

Ordering by predicted output length alone is worse. On conversation, where the
predictor has some signal, SJF-Pred matches the others in $\bar{L}_n$ but
doubles $P_{99}$ (207\,s against 103--104\,s). On code, where it is near chance,
SJF-Pred attains $\bar{L}_n = 23081$ against 16724 for FCFS, with $P_{99}$ of
1882\,s against 271\,s. Optimising a quantity that cannot be estimated adds
ordering noise without compensating benefit, and a static priority from a
near-random key starves whatever it ranks last. CB-SJF-Work avoids this because
the observable prefill term anchors its ranking. This negative result is scoped
to the content-blind setting: it does not evaluate the prompt-reading predictors
of $S^3$~\cite{jin2023s3} or Fu \emph{et al.}~\cite{fu2024ltr}, which we did not
implement and which use a signal ours cannot see.

\subsection{SLO attainment}
CB-SJF-Work roughly doubles SLO attainment over FCFS, 34.5\% against 18.8\%
(conversation) and 15.4\% against 8.6\% (code), matching Oracle-Work to within
0.1 points; SJF-Ctx matches both, so on this metric the entire gain comes from
ordering by context length. Absolute attainment is low, and not because the
replica is saturated: at our lowest load it is still 39.2\% and 17.6\%. A single
unchunked prefill step for a multi-thousand-token prompt consumes much of the
2\,s time-to-first-token budget before any queueing, so the target is
unreachable at any load in this engine configuration. The comparison between
policies remains meaningful; the absolute level should not be read as a claim
about production SLO attainment.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limits}

\input{tables/sensitivity}

\textbf{The cost model is analytical.} We do not have access to the accelerator
hardware these traces were served on, and our coefficients are modelled rather
than measured. The quantity our mechanism depends on, however, is the ratio of
context to output tokens, which is a property of the traces: 15.5:1 and 110.7:1
in aggregate. We therefore stress that ratio directly, by scaling output length,
rather than perturbing a cost coefficient. Scaling output length moves both the
decode share of work and the KV footprint, which is the binding constraint in
our engine; scaling a decode cost coefficient alone leaves the KV footprint
untouched and is uninformative.

\textbf{Longer generations, including reasoning and agentic workloads.} Scaling
output length by up to 16$\times$ (Table~\ref{tab:sens}a) drives the prefill
share of engine time from 75.4\% to 11.2\% on conversation. The improvement over
FCFS does not collapse; it rises, from 46.0\% to 75.1\%, because ordering by
observable work stays a good ordering even when the observable term is no longer
the majority. What degrades is the price of content-blindness: CB-SJF-Work runs
1.08$\times$ the mean normalised latency of Oracle-Work at $1\times$ but
3.76$\times$ at $16\times$ (conversation) and 5.53$\times$ (code).
Correspondingly the learned predictor, worth 0.3\% on today's traces, becomes
worth 21.1\%. For long-generation workloads our conclusions would need
revisiting and prompt-based prediction would likely repay its cost.

\textbf{Prefix caching.} This is the most serious threat and the traces cannot
answer it. Production stacks serve repeated prefixes from cache, and multi-turn
conversation is the case with the highest hit rates, but the traces record
context length and not cache residency. Reducing executed prefill to model a hit
rate, while the scheduler still observes the full context length (cache-hit
length depends on token identity and so is not content-blind), the improvement
over FCFS falls to 12.8\% (conversation) and 14.8\% (code) at a 90\% hit rate
(Table~\ref{tab:sens}b). The recovered oracle gap stays high, 85.5\% and 74.5\%,
because caching shrinks the benefit available to any scheduler rather than to
ours specifically, but the absolute case for work-aware admission weakens
considerably. Prefix-cache-aware content-blind scheduling is the main open
problem this work exposes.

\textbf{Non-preemptive admission.} Our scheduler controls admission order only:
no preemption, swapping or aging. This isolates the effect of admission
information, and it is also why our tails degrade; preemptive
schedulers~\cite{wu2026fastserve,pecsched} address that failure mode. Our
oracles are upper bounds on admission-order policies specifically. We likewise
model a single replica without chunked prefill~\cite{agrawal2024sarathi} or
phase disaggregation~\cite{patel2024splitwise,zhong2024distserve}. Chunking does
not change total prefill work or KV occupancy, which is what binds here, so we
expect the central claim to survive, but we have not verified it. Finally, two
workloads from one provider over one week support a claim about the regime we
measured, not a universal one.

\textbf{Reproducibility.} All experiments use public data. Windows are selected
deterministically, the pipeline is content-hashed to verify byte-identical
reruns, seeds live in one configuration module, and every number in this paper
is generated programmatically from stored result files rather than transcribed.
Code will be released upon acceptance.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked what LLM inference scheduling loses if the scheduler may not read
prompt content. On 44.1M requests of Azure production traffic the answer is:
less than the literature's focus on prompt-based length prediction would
suggest. Prefill, the component observable without reading content, is the
larger part of service work on these traces, and ordering by estimated total
work recovers 79.3\% to 90.3\% of the oracle-attainable improvement in mean
normalised latency and roughly doubles SLO attainment, even on a workload where
output length is statistically unpredictable from metadata.

Three qualifications belong with that result. The gain is paid for in the tail,
by a factor of up to 3.1$\times$, and an oracle scheduler pays the same price,
so it is a property of shortest-first admission rather than of content-blindness.
A learned output-length predictor is not worth deploying on today's traces,
contributing 0.1\% to 1.3\% over ordering by raw context length, though it
becomes worth 21\% once generations are 16$\times$ longer. And prefix caching,
which these traces cannot expose, would shrink the benefit for every policy
studied here.

The practical implication is narrow but useful: on workloads resembling these, a
scheduler that never sees prompt content gives up little, and ordering by
context length alone captures nearly all of it.
The natural next steps are to combine content-blind ordering with preemption or
aging, which our tail results show is needed, and to extend it to
prefix-cache-aware serving.
